Grammar reference

Would

We use would in several common ways: for hypothetical results, advice, cautious language, past habits, wishes and reported forms of will.

ConditionalsAdviceCautious languagePast habitsWish + wouldPast form of will

1. Conditionals

Would introduces a hypothetical result or consequence.

Present / future:
would + infinitive
Past:
would have + past participle
If they interfered, it’d just make a bad situation worse.
I would probably have stayed with him if he’d apologized.
Important: we do not normally use would in the if-clause.
If I would have had more free time → If I had more free time, I’d read a lot more.
If he would’ve told me earlier → If he’d told me earlier, this wouldn’t have happened.

2. Advice

We commonly use I’d (= I would) to give advice.

I’d …I’d advise you to …I’d recommend …-ingI’d suggest …-ingIf I were you, I’d …If I were in your shoes, I’d …
I’d still give him another chance.
I’d consider talking it over with a therapist.
I’d advise you to see a therapist.
I’d recommend taking a class.
If I were you, I’d speak to someone about it.

3. Cautious opinions

Use would to soften an opinion or create distance from what you say.

I’d say …I wouldn’t say …I would have said …I wouldn’t have said …
I’d say he has a stubborn streak.
I wouldn’t say it’s a disaster — just a slight setback.
I wouldn’t have said it’s a big problem.

4. Cautious predictions and requests

Would can make predictions, speculation and requests less direct.

Predictions and speculation

Although he’s a little frail now, I’d expect him to recover well. Less cautious: I expect him to …
I don’t imagine there’d be a problem. Less cautious: there’ll be …
I’d hope to be back by six. Less cautious: I hope to be …
I would think / I would have thought they’d get married.

Polite requests

Would it be OK to leave early?Compare: Is it OK …?
Would you mind helping me?Compare: Do you mind …?

5. Past habits

Use would instead of used to or the past simple for repeated past actions.

When my son was a toddler, I’d do the childcare most days.
Before the course, he’d often get into unnecessary confrontations.
If I’d had a bad day, my gran would always comfort me.
Not for past states:
We would live in Leeds. → We used to live in Leeds.

6. Wish + would

Use it for a present habit or situation that you want to change or stop.

I wish he’d show more commitment.
I wish they wouldn’t interfere.
I wish it would stop raining.
Two restrictions: normally avoid it when the subject is the same, and avoid it for states.
I wish I would be taller. → I wish I was taller.
I wish my parents would live further away. → I wish my parents lived further away.
I wish my boyfriend wouldn’t have that beard. → I wish my boyfriend didn’t have that beard.

7. Past form of will

Use would when reporting speech or thoughts, often after knew, said, thought, promised, threatened and assured.

I knew it would come to no good.
They said it would be miserable today.
She promised she’d come.
He assured me he’d be here on time.
If the event is still in the future: we can keep will: “He promised he’ll give our assignments back tomorrow.”

Reported refusal or failure

Present won’t can express refusal or failure; reported in the past, it becomes wouldn’t.

I asked him to change desks and he wouldn’t — he refused point blank.
The car wouldn’t start this morning.
